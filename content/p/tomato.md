I have a problem with a specific class of _pop facts_ that get thrown around.
It goes like this: someone says, for example, "I love tomatoes, it's a juicy
vegetable", only to be responded with something like "well, you know, it's
technically a fruit".

I think there's a greater issue to resolve before we assign truth values to
things stated by both of these people, and I think the underlying lesson is
much more valuable than having a clean resolution on what category tomato
belongs to.

## Domain specific terminology

It's incorrect to assume terminology from one domain can't mean something very
different in another domain.

It's instantly obvious when you look at an example:

> **[Bug](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bug)** (n.)
> 
> (entomology) An insect of the order Hemiptera (the “true bugs”). 
>
> (informal, proscribed) Any motile small terrestrial invertebrate, especially one that is seen as a pest. 

In computing, the same word is used for something completely unreleated:

> (computing, engineering jargon) A problem that needs fixing.

However, things can get *much* dirtier. Sometimes the terminologies overalap
(there is not a rule that says they can't). And such terms can lead to
confusion.

> **[Metal](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/metal)** (n.)
>
> Any of a number of chemical elements in the periodic table that form a
> metallic bond with other metal atoms; generally shiny, somewhat malleable and
> hard, often a conductor of heat and electricity. 

but:

> (astronomy) An element which was not directly created after the Big Bang but
> instead formed through nuclear reactions; any element other than hydrogen and
> helium.

(I wasn't aware of this unusual usage of the term _metal_ until I looked for
good examples for this article. Thanks to my friend Pranav Mahapatra from
[evincism.com](https://evincism.com/) for validating the term is actually in
common use in this sense.)

## The meaning of a word lies in its use [0]

Words don't exist in isolation, context around them can change what they mean.

I would go as far as saying that different people may use and understand a word
slightly differently in the same discussion. A lot of times, this results in
disagreements: Is [_hijra_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijra_(South_Asia)) a
gender? Yes and no:

> **[Gender](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/gender)** (n.)
>
> [..]
>
> 2. Sex (a category, either male or female)
>
> 3. Identification as a man, a woman, or something else, and association with a
> (social) role or set of behavioral and cultural traits, clothing, etc; a
> category to which a person belongs on this basis.

Neither of the answer is incorrect, they are simply using the word "gender"
differently. And neither of these usages of the term is incorrect either. You
cannot deny that for most of the history gender simply meant _sex_ and
continues to do so. You also cannot refuse, at the same time, that people (and
acadamia) don't use it in the second sense.

When having discourse, I think it's imporant that you clarify in what sense
your opponents are using a word. This video comes to mind: [Jordan Peterson:
"What do you mean by believe"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gujcpG4g9Cw). He
was memed, a lot, by atheists, because he asked "What do you mean by
believe"--I think he was doing something, however much I disagree with his
definition of what it means to "believe", it's how he chooses to use the word.
It's an important clarification however, becuase now the discussion can proceed
with his understanding of what it means to believe in mind.

I think we should do this more often.

## Mutual exclusion

Two categories may not necessarily be mutually exclusive, that is, a thing can
belong to two different categories, _A_ and _B_, unless their definitions
necessarily require them to be disjoint.

This seems obvious, but we do think of some categories as mutually exclusive
when they are not. Consider: water, it's both natual (as in naturally
occuring) and a chemical substance. A lot of people seem to use the term
_chemical_ to mean _synthesised, harmful substances_, which is of course in
mutual exclusion with _natuarally occuring_.

> **[Chemical](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/chemical)** (n.)
>
> 1. (chemistry, sciences) Any specific chemical element or chemical compound
>    or alloy.
>
> 2. (colloquial) An artificial chemical compound. 
>    "I color my hair with henna, not chemicals."
>
> 3. (slang) An addictive drug.

Again, I am not arguing against such usage, but academia uses it in a way that
is not mutually exclusive with _natural_, therefore, as far as chemists are
concerned, water is both natural and a chemical.

Imagine having a conversation where one states "water is a chemical" and the
other person states, with an intention to refute, that water is natural. They
would be right, but it's not the argument the other person think they are
making. Being a natural substance does not imply that water is not chemical. Of
course, their statement is true, but it's not in conflict with the initial
statement the other person made.

## Tomato

I started with tomatoes, but I sidelined them for the rest of the article.

The pop fact that "tomato is a fruit" has it's origins in the botanical definition of a fruit:

> **[fruit](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/fruit)** (n.)
>
> 1. (botany) A product of fertilization in a plant, specifically:
>    * The seed-bearing part of a plant; often edible, colourful, fragrant, and
>      sweet or sour; produced from a floral ovary after fertilization.
>
>    * The spores of cryptogams and their accessory organs.

Tomatoes fit.

However, the same pop fact has now taken the shape of "tomato is a fruit, not
vegetable".

This stems from the fact that in culinary, fruits and vegetables are disjoint
categories.

[You can't eat your cake and have it too.](https://www.crimemuseum.org/crime-library/terrorism/ted-kaczynski-the-unabomber/).

Botanical concept of a "fruit" is about classifying parts a plant. The parts of
plants that are _not_ fruit are: stem, leaf, root.

The category of _vegetable_ is simply not something botany concerns itself
with. Why should it? Whether something is vegetable is of little importance to
a botanist.

To me, saying "tomato is a fruit, not a vegetable" is an idiotic statement to
make. Not because it is false (which it actually is [1]), but because it misses
the point of both, botany and culinary classification of fruits and vegtables.

It reads like the person making the claim does not actually know what a
botanical fruit is--one would never contrast that with a vegetable otherwise.

This "pop fact" is "pop" becuase it spread by people stating it to others
verbatim without actually getting into the nature of those words in botanical
and culinary disciplines, and providing no argument for why something that's a
fruit can't be a vegetable (spoiler: it can).

Furthermore, the culinary classification is culture based and there is no clear
line between fruits and vegetables.

Quoting Wiktionary:

> 2. (culinary) Any sweet or sour, edible part of a plant that resembles
>    seed-bearing fruit (see former sense) even if it does not develop from a
>    floral ovary.
>
>    * A sweet or sweetish vegetable, such as the petioles of rhubarb, that
>      resembles a true fruit or is used in cookery as if it was a fruit.

But the way _I personally_ see people use the term as:

> A part of the plant, shaped like true-fruit, which is eaten without
> cooking.

By this definition, something can be a (culinary) fruit or vegetable depending
on where you live and the culinary norms.

In parts of India, jackfruit is cooked while in others it's eaten raw. That
makes jackfruit a:

* A _botanical_ fruit
* A _culinary_ fruit
* A _culinary_ vegetable

Even at an individual level, for a person who eats tomatos raw (yes I do), it
is a (culinary) fruit.
